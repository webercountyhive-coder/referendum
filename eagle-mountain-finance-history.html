The "No Tax Increase Since 2010" City Was Sending $10–15 Million a Year to Redevelopment Areas Before the Rate Ever Moved

Eagle Mountain held its property-tax rate flat for roughly sixteen years — a fact the city cites often. But its own audited financial statements show a fast-growing amount of the property tax it collects never reaches the General Fund at all. It goes to redevelopment project areas first.

The short version

  • Eagle Mountain held its property-tax rate flat for years before 2026's 69.81% increase.
  • The city's own audited reports show its Redevelopment Agency (RDA) Fund took in $10.46 million in FY2024 and $15.03 million in FY2025. That is property-tax increment from project areas that does not reach the General Fund.
  • The RDA Fund's share grew from about 34% to about 40% of all city tax revenue in one year.
  • Utah County's separate Report 700 for tax year 2023 puts the increment across Eagle Mountain's five known project areas at about $10.44 million, a close match.
  • This is legal and disclosed in public filings. No record located shows a city fund pledged to back a CRA/RDA bond, but bond disclosure filings were not yet checked.

New Here? The Short Version

Eagle Mountain officials point to roughly sixteen years without a Truth-in-Taxation rate increase as proof of fiscal restraint — right up until this year's 69.81% hike. That's true of the rate. But the city's own audited financial statements show something the rate history doesn't capture: a Redevelopment Agency fund that pulled in $10.5 million in FY2024 and $15.0 million in FY2025 — property tax increment captured from redevelopment project areas before it ever reaches the General Fund, the budget that pays for police, roads, and parks. That's not a hidden tax increase; it's money the city, the school district, the water district, and the fire service would otherwise have received, redirected instead to fund development inside specific project areas. It's legal, disclosed in public filings, and growing fast — and it's part of the answer to "why does this city need more money now."

1. The Rate That Stayed Flat

Eagle Mountain's certified property-tax rate sat near 0.00053–0.00056 for years before 2026's Resolution R-62-2026 (covered in the companion referendum case file). The city's own documents don't agree on exactly how long: its FY2023 and FY2024 audited financial reports say the rate hadn't gone through Truth in Taxation "since FY2005," while its FY2025 report and its own Truth-in-Taxation web page say "since 2010." That five-year discrepancy appears in the city's own official filings and is unresolved — worth a direct question to the Finance Department.

What's fair to the city here A flat certified rate for over a decade is real, and it's the metric Truth in Taxation is built around. The story below isn't that the rate history is false — it's that a flat rate and a growing General Fund can coexist with a separate, fast-growing diversion of that same growth into redevelopment project areas. Both things are true at once.

3. The Gap Between "City-Wide" and "General Fund"

This is the central finding of this piece. Eagle Mountain's audited financial statements report two totals each year that belong side by side: the city's total tax revenue across every fund (property, sales and franchise tax together), and the property tax that specifically lands in the Redevelopment Agency (RDA) Fund — increment captured from CRA/RDA project areas before it reaches the General Fund. The RDA amount is property tax the General Fund, Alpine School District, the water district, and the fire service never see.

Both the RDA Fund's tax revenue ($10,457,606 in FY2024; $15,025,376 in FY2025) and the all-funds "Taxes" totals are stated directly in Eagle Mountain's own ACFRs — not estimated. The RDA Fund's share grew from roughly 34% to roughly 40% of all city tax revenue in a single year. Measured against property tax alone, its share would be larger.

Correction and reading note (Oct. 1, 2026) In Eagle Mountain's audited statements, the $30.67M (FY2024) and $37.53M (FY2025) totals are the "Taxes" line for all governmental funds. That line combines property, sales, and franchise taxes — the FY2024 property, sales, and franchise figures in Section 2 ($13.18M + $11.12M + $6.35M) add up to about the same $30.65M. So the 34% and 40% shares above are shares of all city tax revenue, not of property tax alone. An earlier version of this page labeled them as shares of property tax; the labels were corrected Oct. 1, 2026. The dollar amounts did not change. The ACFR's own notes say tax increment "revenues are recorded as revenues of the RDA," so the RDA money is property tax. Measured against property tax alone, the RDA's share would be larger, not smaller. How this was read: the FY2024 and FY2025 ACFR PDFs on eaglemountain.gov, read by text extraction, not rendered pages.
Finding The RDA Fund's tax-increment capture grew 44% in one year — from $10.46M in FY2024 to $15.03M in FY2025 — while the council was simultaneously telling residents the city needed a 69.81% property-tax increase to cover a sheriff's contract. Both can be true (the RDA money is earmarked for specific project-area obligations, not general spending) but the scale and growth rate of the diversion is directly relevant context the city hasn't volunteered in its own public messaging about the rate increase.

An independent cross-check

Utah County's own Report 700 for tax year 2023 — a separate, county-administered accounting, not produced by Eagle Mountain itself — sums the increment captured by all taxing entities (city, Alpine School District, Central Utah Water Conservancy District, fire service) across Eagle Mountain's five known project areas to roughly $10.44 million for that tax year. That lines up closely with the ACFR's $10,457,606 RDA Fund figure for the following fiscal year — independent corroboration from a different government office, using a different accounting method, of the same order of magnitude.

5. Risk Framework: What Kind of Exposure Is This?

Not every tax-increment mechanism carries the same kind of risk to city finances. Sorting them matters more than treating "there's a CRA" as an alarm by itself.

Tier 1 — Redirects money indirectly

Confirmed, real, ongoing revenue loss to the General Fund and other taxing entities — but no direct bond obligation sits on the city's own books. This is not "protected" or risk-free; it is money the public services budget does not get, every year, by design.

Applies to: all five known project areas. $10.4M–$15.0M/year and growing.

Tier 2 — Untested

No public document answers what happens to city obligations if the underlying development declines in value, changes ownership, or a private financing arrangement secured against future increment defaults.

Applies to: Sweetwater #1 / #1 Phase 2 and Pole Canyon CRA, given ~$1.39B in combined increment value; also the $220M Water & Sewer Revenue Bond, Series 2026 (likely non-recourse to the General Fund by structure, but the bond resolution itself wasn't obtained to confirm it).

Tier 3 — Confirmed direct risk

An official has stated, on the record, that a specific city fund is pledged or serves as fallback for a CRA/RDA/PID bond. This is the only tier that would mean General Fund tax dollars are directly on the hook.

No Tier 3 finding as of this research (Sept. 17, 2026). That reflects the absence of documents located — EMMA/MSRB bond disclosure filings and RDA interlocal agreements were not yet checked — not a confirmed absence of risk.

6. What's Missing — Said Plainly

  • Report 700 for Eagle Mountain was only located for tax year 2023. 2022, 2024, and 2025 versions could not be found at the expected URL pattern, and the county's online redevelopment portal appears to require selecting the agency from a form rather than a direct link. Update, Oct. 1, 2026: the 2022 report was since located at a different address (linked in Sources); 2024 and 2025 are still not located.
  • Each ACFR's statistical section — the 10-year tables of taxable value and tax rates that would settle the FY2023–FY2025 taxable-value question outright — exists in the table of contents but could not be retrieved in full.
  • The actual text of Resolution R-62-2026 was not obtained directly; its terms are corroborated by the city's own Truth-in-Taxation page and independent reporting, but the source document itself should be requested from the City Recorder or pulled from the city's CivicClerk portal.
  • "Sweetwater CRA #2" is referenced elsewhere but unconfirmed here.
  • EMMA/MSRB bond disclosure filings for any Eagle Mountain or Eagle Mountain RDA bonds were not checked — this is the correct place to look to settle the Tier 3 question one way or the other.
